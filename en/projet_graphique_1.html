Custom sprite creation in Super Metroid

Full recreation of Samus animation sets for Sans, Inkling Girl and Samus Maid, while respecting the visual and technical constraints of Super Metroid on SNES.

Role
Pixel artist & ROM modder
Context
Personal project
Year
2024
Type
Pixel art & game modding

Starting point

Super Metroid (SNES, 1994) is a landmark of 2D exploration games, known for the readability of its animation, the precision of its movement and the strength of its art direction despite the technical limits of its era.

That exact framework is what interested me: fully replacing the main character’s visual identity while preserving the readability and rhythm of the original game.

Modding context

The project sits within the Super Metroid modding community, especially around ROM hacks, VARIA Randomizer and Map Rando, where routes are fast, unpredictable and replayed at a high level.

VARIA Randomizer mostly changes item placement and the organization of the main map areas, while Map Rando rebuilds the map room by room. In both cases, routes become less predictable and sprite readability becomes even more important.

In that context, a custom sprite must stay immediately readable in motion, including during very fast sequences or frequent direction changes.

The project did not stay at the level of a personal exercise: some of my sprites were also reused by players in the community during actual games and Map Rando runs, including at times by OatsnGoats. That is a strong indicator of readability and real in-game integration beyond how a sprite sheet looks in isolation.

Sprites designed for the Super Metroid modding ecosystem, all the way to real Map Rando runs.

What I produced

I recreated the full sprite set of the base character (Samus) for three finalized variants:

  • Sans (Undertale)
  • Inkling Girl (Splatoon)
  • Samus Maid (stylized variant)

The work covers the main poses (idle, run, jump, aim, shots, morphball, shinespark and more), with animation adaptations designed to preserve the rhythm of the original game.

The goal was not to modify only one part of the character: I replaced the entire Samus animation set for each variant.

Each variant represents 582 sprites replaced and adjusted on its own.

Project challenge

The challenge was not only graphic. I had to fit characters with very different proportions, silhouettes and visual codes into an already defined animation system, without losing the immediate readability that makes Super Metroid so strong in motion.

One animation set, three characters: adaptation of poses, silhouettes and special actions.

SNES technical constraints

The design had to respect strict limits tied to the console:

  • Sprite size and tile-based breakdown
  • A palette limited to 16 colors including 1 transparent color, so 15 visible colors
  • No added animation slots: I had to strictly respect the vanilla slots, such as 4 idle frames, 8 run frames and 1 shinespark frame
  • Immediate readability on short animation cycles
  • Visual coherence across every action frame

That framework forces precise simplification choices: silhouette, contrast and timing matter more than decorative detail.

Suit palettes

Super Metroid applies color variations depending on the suit. To remain coherent with the game, I worked with dedicated palettes for Power, Varia and Gravity.

The palette below shows the colors used for the three suits, excluding the transparent pixel, followed by a visual reference of the three original Samus versions.

Creation pipeline (Aseprite)

I used Aseprite to build the sprites frame by frame, relying on references from each universe so I could preserve the character identity while respecting the visual grammar of Super Metroid.

  • Build of the key poses first, then in-betweens
  • Loop checks in the actual rhythm of the game
  • Custom palette and shading work for each suit: Power Suit, Varia Suit, Gravity Suit
  • Scale adjustments to match Samus’s body template in Super Metroid, for example Sans is small in his original game, but his combat sprite is too large to be reused as-is
  • Palette adjustments to preserve readability in motion
  • In-game integration and testing with SpriteSomething to inject the sprites into Super Metroid

In-game testing was essential: a sprite that looks strong on a sheet or inside Aseprite can become unreadable once it is pushed into the real rhythm of Super Metroid. Validation therefore had to happen through animation, not only through still images.

Example of a special action adapted in-game: run, aim, morph and shinespark.

Creative adaptation examples

Some animations forced me to adapt iconic movements or visual signs from each universe into the existing slots of Super Metroid, a bit like how Super Smash Bros reinterprets the emblematic gestures of a series within its own combat system.

  • For Inkling Girl, I used the human / squid transformation idea for the morphball
  • For Inkling Girl’s shinespark, I adapted the Kraken special attack to the framework imposed by Super Metroid
  • For Sans, Samus’s arm cannon is replaced by a Gaster Blaster, and the shinespark is reinterpreted with bone visuals inspired by how he attacks in Undertale
  • On run, jump and aiming poses, I simplified some volumes to preserve instant readability despite the small display size

What this project helped me develop

This work taught me how to create within a highly constrained framework, validating every choice directly in-game rather than from a static sprite sheet. It helped me improve on:

  • Analyzing and rebuilding existing animations without breaking their rhythm
  • Mastering retro technical constraints such as palette, size, contrast and readability
  • Maintaining visual coherence across very different universes inside one game
  • Frame-by-frame execution precision
